Phase 2 · Neural models

What the models learned

Alongside the lab, the project trains small computer models that learn the way the paper describes: by guessing what will happen next, while their senses start blurry and sharpen with age. This page explains what they learned, without the jargon.

The idea in four sentences

We built a simple simulated nursery: a toy that moves, a baby's hand that waves, a screen that hides things, and sounds. One small network per sense (sight, hearing, touch, body position) watches this nursery and learns to predict the next moment. Its input starts as blurry, muffled and noisy as a newborn's and is sharpened in steps to match a 3-, 6- and 12-month-old. A further network combines all the senses.

Four findings

1

Do the senses mature in the baby order?

Yes, but we built that in

Touch and body sense work first, hearing improves next, vision comes last. That order comes from the input settings, so it is a check, not a discovery. The models also learn more slowly than the blurry input would allow.

2

Does a blurry start help?

Partly

It helps hearing a little and costs vision a little. Letting the network itself grow over time halves the training effort but slows learning.

3

Do the models know a hidden toy is still there?

No

Babies look longer when a hidden toy magically vanishes. No vision model was "surprised". The combined model seemed surprised, but only because the toy's sound gave it away.

4

Are senses better together?

Yes

Combining senses finds a fully hidden toy better than any single sense.

What this does and does not tell you about a real baby

The models are tiny and live in a simple, made-up world. They show which baby-like patterns follow from baby-like input alone (the order of the senses) and which need something more (expecting hidden things to persist). They do not measure or predict any real baby. The planned next step, training on head-camera recordings of real children and testing against an infant-reasoning benchmark, has not been done yet.

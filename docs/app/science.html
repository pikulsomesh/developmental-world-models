How we know

Where this comes from

Everything on this site is built from published studies of groups of babies, and the same numbers were used to train small computer models that learn from baby-like senses.

The numbers

Researchers measure a baby's sight by showing stripes and watching where the baby looks, or by recording brain responses. They measure hearing by finding the quietest sound a baby turns to. We collected these measurements for each sense from birth to two years and turned them into settings for blur, faded contrast, muted colour and muffled sound. Touch and body sense are known less precisely, so those settings are rougher.

What the computer models showed

We built tiny computer models, one per sense, that learn by guessing what they will sense next. Each was given input filtered like a baby's at each age, in a simple cartoon world with one toy. A few things stood out.

Blurry, faint input slows learning

A model given newborn-like eyesight took much longer to learn where the toy was than the information in its input allowed. Faint contrast mattered more than blur.

Senses work better together

When the toy was hidden, combining hearing, touch and sight kept track of it better than sight alone.

Sensing is not understanding

The models could keep track of a hidden toy, but they were not "surprised" when it vanished, as babies in lab studies often are. Knowing where something is and expecting it to still be there are different.

Early experience leaves traces

A stretch of very blurry input at the start had a lasting effect on the model's eye for fine detail. The same stretch later on did not.

These are small computer models in a simple simulated world, not babies. The findings describe that setting and may not hold in larger models or for real infants.

What this site cannot do

It cannot test a baby. Screens are not calibrated, the age settings describe averages, and every baby develops at their own pace. The pictures show the limits of a baby's eyes and ears, not what the baby experiences, because their brains fill in a great deal.

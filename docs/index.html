A small, open experiment · Phase 0

Do AI models learn like humans?

Sometimes. When a model sees, hears and feels the world the way an infant does, some infant patterns appear on their own. Others do not.

Newborns see a blur, hear muffled sound and feel a lot. Their senses sharpen over the first year, each on its own clock. We gave each sense its own small "world model", fed it the world as an infant of a given age would receive it, and watched what it learned. This page lets you see what each model sees, how it changes with age, and where it does and does not behave like a baby.

Under the hood

How it works

Every model is small enough to train on a laptop CPU. The full derivations are in the Methods of the paper.

1 · A developmental front end per sense

Each sense s sees the clean observation through a fixed, age-indexed operator. For vision, blur, then contrast, then colour:

cpd = acuity in cycles per degree, CS = contrast sensitivity. Hearing uses frequency blur, a high-frequency roll-off and reduced left/right difference; touch uses receptive-field blur.

2 · A world model per sense

An encoder e maps what the sense delivers to a latent state; a dynamics model f predicts the next state from the last k states and the infant's own movement u; a decoder d maps back.

3 · Growing without forgetting

A growing model widens each hidden layer from n to n' units. New units get random input weights and zero output weights, so the network computes exactly the same function right after growth:

The catch: the gradient reaching the new input weights R is multiplied by the zero output weights, so new capacity does nothing until those move. That is one reason growth slowed learning here.

4 · Measuring surprise

Surprise is the prediction error S around the moment the toy should reappear. The violation-of-expectation index compares the impossible and possible movies:

Why vision fails: a model trained with squared error predicts the average future. When the reappearance time is uncertain, the average future behind a screen is mostly "screen", so the error-minimising forecast is that nothing reappears.

Three lenses

Mathematics, computer science, neuroscience

Mathematics

A front end is a channel: by the data-processing inequality no model can recover more about the toy than I(\tilde o;\,y). Squared-error prediction converges to the conditional mean, which explains copy-like forecasts under occlusion. Function-preserving growth is an exact identity, but its zero-initialised gradients are a saddle the optimiser must leave.

Computer science

Immature input is a curriculum, like a continuation method that solves a smoothed problem first. Shuffling the same inputs keeps most of the benefit in hearing, which points to data augmentation more than order. Growth halves training compute (parameter × updates) but costs accuracy at matched updates.

Neuroscience

The front ends stand for the eye's optics and cone maturation, the cochlea and binaural brainstem circuits, and skin receptive fields. The brain adds synapses and then prunes them, rather than adding neurons; plasticity closes on per-sense clocks. Fusion stands for multisensory areas such as the superior colliculus and superior temporal sulcus.

What this does not show

  • Everything is synthetic: a 32×32 world with one toy, a hand and a screen. It is a mechanism test, not a model of real infants.
  • Vision filters follow published infant data; the hearing, touch and body-sense tables are placeholders that respect the known order of maturation. So "the senses mature in the infant order" is put there by those tables.
  • "Months" are training stages, not hours of experience.
  • Probes show what is decodable, not what the model does. Infant tests measure behaviour, which is why hide-and-seek matters.
  • Three seeds per regime: differences smaller than the bands are not established.
  • We reviewed our own report as a referee would; the review and our responses are on GitHub.

Approach

How the study works

Immaturity is put in the input, not in the network: each sense sees the world through a fixed filter set by age, and a learning network sits behind it.

A simulated nursery

A toy that can be seen, heard and felt moves about; the infant's own hand babbles; in half the scenes a screen hides the toy. Each sense receives its own stream.

An age filter for each sense

Vision is blurred, faded and desaturated using published infant acuity and contrast data; hearing is quieter, muffled and less directional; touch is coarser; body position is noisier. The filters are fixed, not learned.

A world model for each sense

Behind each filter a network learns by predicting its next input from the recent past and the hand's movement. Training runs through four stages named 0, 3, 6 and 12 months (names for stages, not real time). In some regimes the network is widened between stages without changing what it computes.

Fusion

A fusion world model combines the senses' internal states and learns to predict across them, including filling in a sense that is switched off.

What is compared

Six training regimes see the same data with the same training budget, so differences come from the schedule or from growth.

What is measured

What is stored

A linear read-out fitted to the model's internal state, scored by R². It measures what is decodable, not what the model uses.

What the input allows

An information ceiling: a separate network trained directly on the task from the same filtered input.

What is expected

Surprise (forecast error) when a hidden toy vanishes compared with when it reappears, as in infant violation-of-expectation studies.

The theory in brief

Paper 1 proves four statements for simplified settings: filtered input bounds what any representation can know; for a linear read-out under gradient flow, contrast sets the speed of learning and blur the ceiling; widening a network without changing its function delays learning; and a squared-error forecaster is more surprised by reappearance than by disappearance.

Limits

Everything is synthetic and small, with one toy and low-resolution scenes, and results rest on linear read-outs and 3 to 5 seeds per condition. The networks are not trained on real video, the model ages are not calibrated to hours of infant experience, and taste, smell and balance are not modelled. Next steps are head-camera recordings from children, an embodied infant simulator, and comparisons with infant behaviour on the same tasks.

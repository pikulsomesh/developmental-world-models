The two papers

One paper for machine learning and one for developmental science. They share the simulation and the age filters and say where they share data.

Paper 1 · Machine learning

Learning Like Infants: Input Constraints in Predictive World Models

Infants learn from input that is degraded, ordered in time and delivered by senses that mature at different rates, whereas machine-learning systems are usually trained on clean, shuffled data. We ask how such constraints change learning in predictive world models. We prove that, for a linear read-out under gradient flow, input contrast sets the speed of learning while blur lowers the reachable ceiling; that widening a network delays learning; and that a squared-error forecaster is more surprised by an object's reappearance than by its disappearance. We test these results in a controlled multisensory simulation with one world model per sense and 5 seeds per regime. In this small synthetic setting, the quality and order of input change learning in predictable ways, which suggests, as a hypothesis for tests at scale, that they could be a cheap lever for training world models when data are limited.

Paper 2 · Developmental science

Simulating Infant Senses with Age-Tuned Networks

What a newborn sees, hears and feels, and how this changes month by month, has been measured sense by sense, with different methods and in different units. We bring these measurements together for the first two years of life and ask whether small networks tuned to a given age can turn them into a quick, inspectable simulation of sensory perception. Each sense's immaturity is written as a fixed, age-indexed filter, and a small prediction network learns behind it. The filters alone reproduce the order in which the senses mature, by construction. The networks add what filters cannot: in vision they extract far less than their input allows at early stages, and a critical period and perceptual narrowing emerge without being built in; in hearing they track what their input allows. The same filters drive the Baby Senses Lab, a research and teaching tool, not a diagnostic test.

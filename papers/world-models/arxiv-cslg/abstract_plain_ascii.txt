Predictive world models are usually trained on clean, shuffled data at full quality from the first update. We study how learning changes when the training stream is instead degraded, staged over time and split across modalities. We model each constraint as a fixed degradation operator applied to one modality and derive four short results: an information bound on what any representation of filtered input can retain; an exact learning-time law for a linear read-out under gradient flow, in which scaling input contrast by a factor kappa delays learning by a factor of one over kappa squared while Gaussian blur mainly lowers the reachable ceiling; a plateau after function-preserving widening, because new units receive gradient only through their zero-initialised outgoing weights; and a conditional-mean account showing that a squared-error forecaster is more surprised by an occluded object's reappearance than by its disappearance when reappearance is uncertain. We test each result on small Adam-trained networks in a synthetic multisensory environment, with one predictive world model per modality (convolutional encoder, residual latent dynamics, spatial-broadcast decoder) and a fusion model trained with random modality masking, across six training curricula and two front-end ablations, 5 seeds each. The most degraded stage slows the linear solution 39-fold, and the trained vision model reaches position R-squared 0.02 where a supervised network on the same input reaches 0.60; the ablations attribute this lag to contrast, not blur. A degraded-to-clean curriculum costs vision a little and helps audio localisation, mainly as augmentation; network growth halves parameter-updates but slows learning; and fusion tracks an occluded object better than any single modality. All results are from a small synthetic setting; transfer to larger models and real data is untested.
